1 · The data

Sixty-four points on a plane

Imagine a dataset of 64 examples. Each example is a point with two features, drawn here as coordinates x₁ (horizontal) and x₂ (vertical), and a label we want to predict.

1 · The data

Two classes: blue and orange

Every point belongs to one of two classes. In your course project this could be “male vs. female name”, “positive vs. negative review” — here it is just a color. The features are the measurements; the label is the answer.

1 · The data

Can a few yes/no questions separate them?

Look at the picture: the blues and the oranges live in different corners. A good learning algorithm should capture that structure — not with one complicated formula, but with a handful of simple questions like “is x₁ ≤ 5?”.

1 · The data

Clusters, not chaos

There is a little noise in the middle, but the overall pattern is clear: two clusters per class. Keep this shape in mind — everything that follows (splits, boundaries, forests) is about carving this plane into regions that are as pure as possible.

2 · Inference

Prediction = walking down a tree

A trained decision tree is a flowchart. To classify a new point, you start at the root and answer each question on the way down. Watch the highlighted point travel to its leaf.

2 · Inference

Question 1: “is x₁ ≤ 5?”

The root asks its question. Our point has x₁ < 5, so it follows the yes branch to the left child. On the scatter, the left half is now shaded — everything in that region took the same first step.

2 · Inference

Question 2: “is x₂ ≤ 5?”

The next node asks about the other feature. Again the answer is yes, and the point moves one level deeper. The shaded region shrinks to the bottom-left rectangle.

2 · Inference

A leaf gives the answer

The bottom-left region is pure blue, so the leaf predicts blue. That is inference: a path of questions, ending in a majority vote of the training points that fell into the leaf. No gradients, no matrices — just counting.

3 · Gini impurity

How mixed is this node?

To choose the best question, the training algorithm needs a ruler for “mixedness”. One classic ruler is Gini impurity. Look at the node: it contains 10 points, 8 blue and 2 orange.

3 · Gini impurity

\(G = 1 - \sum_k p_k^2\)

The class probabilities are p = (0.8, 0.2), so

\(G = 1 - (0.8^2 + 0.2^2) = 1 - 0.68 = \mathbf{0.32}\)

A moderately mixed node: not pure, not maximally mixed.

3 · Gini impurity

Perfectly balanced → maximum mess

\(G = 1 - (0.5^2 + 0.5^2) = \mathbf{0.5}\)

With two classes, 0.5 is the largest Gini possible. If the node is a coin flip, no question has been answered yet.

3 · Gini impurity

Pure → zero, and we stop

\(G = 1 - (1^2 + 0^2) = \mathbf{0}\)

A pure node is a leaf: every point agrees, so the majority vote is unanimous. Purity is one of the stop conditions of the training recursion.

3 · Gini impurity

The Gini curve

Plot G(p) = 2p(1−p) for the blue fraction p: it is a parabola peaking at p = 0.5. Intuition: G is the probability that two points drawn at random from the node have different labels — that is exactly what a good split wants to reduce.

4 · Entropy

Entropy measures the same mix — in bits

Information theory has its own ruler: entropy, \(H = -\sum_k p_k \cdot \log_2 p_k\) It answers: “how many yes/no questions, on average, do I still need to identify the label?”

4 · Entropy

Same node, different units

For the 8-blue / 2-orange node:

\(H = -(0.8\log_2 0.8 + 0.2\log_2 0.2) = \mathbf{0.72}\ \text{bits}\)

Compare: 5/5 gives 1 bit (maximum for two classes — one question still needed), and a pure node gives 0 bits: no questions left, the answer is certain.

4 · Entropy

The entropy curve

\(H(p) = -p\log_2 p - (1-p)\log_2(1-p)\) peaks at p = 0.5 with exactly 1 bit — a fair coin needs one yes/no question. The log₂ is what makes the unit “bits”.

4 · Entropy

Gini vs. entropy in practice

Both curves are concave and peak in the middle, so both criteria usually pick the same splits. In practice: CART uses Gini (cheaper to compute — no logarithms), ID3/C4.5 use entropy (information gain). When splitting a node, the algorithm keeps the question with the largest gain: \(\text{Gain} = I(\text{parent}) - \left[\tfrac{n_L}{n}I(L) + \tfrac{n_R}{n}I(R)\right]\)

5 · Training

The algorithm is a recursion

Training a tree means running the same procedure on every child — that is the recursion:

train(node, data, depth):
  if data pure OR depth = max_depth OR too few samples:
    make LEAF, predict majority class   ← stop
  else:
    find question with highest gain
    split data into left / right
    train(left child), train(right child) ← recurse
5 · Training

At each node: scan all questions

“Every question” means every feature and every threshold between sorted values. Watch the candidate line sweep across x₁ — each position is scored by its gain…

5 · Training

…and the best one wins

The vertical cut at x₁ ≈ 5 has the highest gain (Gini drops from 0.5 to a low weighted average), so the root commits to it:

\(\text{Gain} = G(\text{parent}) - \left[\tfrac{n_L}{n}G(L) + \tfrac{n_R}{n}G(R)\right]\)
5 · Training

Then recurse: same procedure, smaller regions

The left child now scans horizontal cuts inside the left half. The right child will scan its own half. Each call of train is independent — only the data region differs.

5 · Training

Stop conditions end the recursion

Every branch stops when it hits a leaf condition: pure node (Gini = 0), max depth reached, or too few samples to split. These three guards are what keep a tree from growing forever — remember them when we talk about overfitting.

6 · Decision boundary

Depth 1: one question, one straight cut

After training a depth-1 tree, color every point of the plane by the prediction of the leaf it falls into. One question produced one vertical line — the decision boundary.

6 · Decision boundary

Depth 2: rectangular regions

Each additional level cuts existing regions again. Two levels → up to four axis-parallel rectangles, each labeled by its majority class.

6 · Decision boundary

Depth 6: a staircase hugging the clusters

A deeper tree produces a staircase of rectangles that wraps around the clusters. Notice the fundamental limitation: trees can only draw lines parallel to the axes, because each question tests a single feature.

6 · Decision boundary

The boundary is the model

Everything a decision tree “knows” is encoded in this partition of the plane. Later models (SVMs, neural nets) can draw slanted or curved boundaries — trees cannot. That simplicity is exactly what makes them easy to understand, and what random forests exploit.

7 · Overfitting

On clean data, a deep tree looks great

This is the same dataset as before. A depth-6 tree separates the clusters and reaches 100% training accuracy. So far, so good.

7 · Overfitting

Now add four mislabeled outliers

Real data is noisy. These four crosses have labels that contradict their neighborhood (a blue point among oranges, an orange among blues).

7 · Overfitting

The deep tree memorizes the noise

Watch the boundary contort: the depth-6 tree carves tiny islands around each outlier to classify them correctly. Training accuracy is still 100% — but the tree has learned accidents, not structure.

7 · Overfitting

The gap between train and fresh data

We test both trees (depth 6 vs. depth 2) on 200 fresh points drawn from the same clusters. The numbers below are computed live: the deep tree wins on the training set but loses on fresh data — that gap is overfitting. The shallow tree generalizes better. This is why we always evaluate on held-out data, and why we cap the depth.

8 · Random forest

One tree is brittle — many trees vote

A single tree depends on the exact sample it was trained on: change a few points and the boundary changes. Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) fixes this: draw many random resamples of the training data and train one tree on each.

8 · Random forest

Bootstrap: sample with replacement

Each tree gets n points drawn with replacement from the original n — so some points appear twice (darker dots below) and some are left out entirely (the “out-of-bag” points, ~37%). Each resample is a slightly different world.

8 · Random forest

Each tree sees a different world

On each resample we train a tree — and at every split we also consider only a random subset of the features (that is the “random” in random forest). The small multiples below show five such trees’ boundaries: similar, but not identical.

8 · Random forest

Majority vote smooths the boundary

The forest predicts by majority vote of all trees (here 25). The big plot shows the forest boundary on the full plane: smoother and more robust than any single tree. The statistics behind the vote — many independent-ish classifiers averaging out — is the whole idea: the wisdom of crowds, with trees.

9 · Boosting & AdaBoost

A 1-D problem: one feature only

Boosting builds trees sequentially, each one fixing the previous ones’ mistakes. To see it clearly, use a single feature x: points with label −1 (bottom row) on the left, +1 (top row) on the right — plus two crossing outliers.

9 · Boosting & AdaBoost

Round 1: the best “stump” (depth-1 tree)

Every point starts with weight 1/n. AdaBoost finds the single threshold with the smallest weighted error ε. Its vote strength is \(\alpha = \tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(\tfrac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\right)\) — a more accurate stump gets a bigger say.

9 · Boosting & AdaBoost

Misclassified points get heavier

Each weight is multiplied by e−α·y·h(x): wrong answers grow (bigger circles), right answers shrink. After renormalizing, the two outliers now carry most of the mass — the next stump will have to get them right.

9 · Boosting & AdaBoost

Rounds 2…T: focus on the hard cases

Repeat: pick the stump minimizing weighted error, compute its α, reweight. Each new vertical line targets where the current ensemble is still wrong.

9 · Boosting & AdaBoost

Final prediction: sign of the weighted vote

The ensemble sums all votes: \(F(x) = \sum_t \alpha_t\, h_t(x), \qquad \hat{y} = \mathrm{sign}(F(x))\) The staircase below is F(x); where it crosses zero is the ensemble’s decision boundary. Many weak stumps, weighted by accuracy, form one strong classifier.

9 · Recap

Bagging vs. boosting

Bagging (random forest): trees in parallel on bootstrap samples — averages away variance (unstable trees). Boosting (AdaBoost): trees in series, each reweighted toward past errors — reduces bias (weak learners). Same ingredient — decision trees — two very different recipes.
